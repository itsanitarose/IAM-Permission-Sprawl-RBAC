# 03. Segregation of Duties (SoD)

<br>

## 1. Investigating Conflicting Access

After reviewing the assigned permissions, I wanted to understand something beyond who had excessive access: **what could someone actually do with those permissions combined?**

I focused on three questions. Could someone create a false payee, redirect a payment, or approve a transaction that benefits them without anyone else checking?

<br>

| Conflicting Permissions | What Could Go Wrong | Recommended Control |
|---|---|---|
| Create new payee + Approve payments | Potential financial fraud or embezzlement. Someone could create a fake supplier using bank details they control, then approve payments for work that was never done. | Require independent approval of new payees and prevent users from approving payments to payees they created. |
| Edit patient billing details + Approve payments | If the system allows changes to payment amounts or recipient details, someone could manipulate those details and approve the payment themselves, potentially directing funds to themselves or someone they know. | Check which billing details can be changed and require someone else to review any payment-related changes before approving the payment. |
| System admin + Financial permissions | If admin rights allow changes to permissions or transaction records, someone could misuse financial access and potentially cover their tracks. | Separate financial duties from administration, independently approve privileged changes, and protect audit logs. |

These combinations stood out because **the risk isn't always in a single permission. It's what becomes possible when one person controls multiple stages of a transaction.**

<br>

## 2. The Billing Supervisor Exception

One combination needed a closer look.

Billing Supervisors are expected to create invoices and approve refunds. Removing either permission would conflict with the supplied job requirements.

But what stops someone from creating an invoice and approving a refund against it?

If the application allows refunds without a verified payment, or permits money to be sent to a different recipient, that combination could potentially be abused to divert company funds.

Before recommending changes, I would check whether the system confirms that the original payment was received before allowing a refund, and whether the refund must go back to the same account or card used to make that payment.

<br>

**My proposed controls:**

- Prevent supervisors from approving refunds against invoices they created.
- Require refunds to be linked to verified payments and returned to the original payment method, with independently approved exceptions.
- Require independent approval for refunds above an agreed threshold.
- Monitor repeated refunds, unusual recipients, and refunds without matching payments.

The goal isn't to remove access that the business needs. It's to make sure legitimate responsibilities don't allow one person to initiate and approve a questionable transaction unchecked.

<br>

## 3. My Assessment

The concern isn't simply that employees have too many permissions.

It's whether those permissions allow someone to **create a financial transaction, approve it, and potentially benefit from it without independent oversight.**

My recommendations focus on breaking that chain through separation of duties, independent approvals, and controls over sensitive financial actions.

These are potential misuse scenarios based on the supplied access records, not evidence that fraud occurred or confirmation that every described action is possible in the application.
